Answering a quiz is not just a way to measure what you know. It is also one of the most effective ways to learn. Research in cognitive psychology has shown it again and again: testing yourself helps you remember better than rereading. Here is why, and how to make the most of it, whether you are studying for an exam or simply want to know everything about your favorite series.

## The testing effect

In 2006, psychologists Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke compared two groups of students who had to remember a text. The first group reread it several times. The second read it once, then tried to recall it without looking at it. In the short term, the rereaders did slightly better. A week later, the trend was clearly reversed: those who had tested themselves remembered much more.

This phenomenon has a name: the testing effect, or retrieval practice. Each time you fetch a piece of information from your memory, you strengthen the path that leads to it. Rereading gives a feeling of familiarity, but does not exercise that path. That is why a text reread ten times can feel mastered… until the moment you have to recall it.

## The forgetting curve

At the end of the 19th century, Hermann Ebbinghaus studied on himself how fast we forget. His conclusion, the forgetting curve, is simple: we forget very quickly at first, then more slowly. Something learned the day before can be largely gone a few days later if you do not go over it again.

The good news is that each review flattens the curve. A piece of information reviewed several times, at longer and longer intervals, ends up settling in for good.

## Spaced repetition

That is the principle of spaced repetition: review an idea just before you forget it, then wait a little longer before the next review. The Leitner system, devised in the 1970s by the German journalist Sebastian Leitner, applies it with boxes of flashcards. A card you get right moves to a box you review less often. A card you get wrong goes back to the first box.

You do not need boxes to apply this idea. It is enough to come back to the same topic a few days later rather than reviewing everything in one go.

## Why mistakes are useful

Getting something wrong is not a failure, as long as you understand why. Studies on the subject show that a mistake followed by an immediate correction is remembered very well. That is why every KamiQuiz question is followed by an explanation: the right answer alone is not enough, you need to understand what makes it right.

A mistake like this also creates a small surprise, and surprise helps memory. You remember for a long time a question you were sure about and got wrong.

## Mixing topics

One last tip: alternate subjects rather than working through them in blocks. Researchers call this interleaving. Mixing categories forces you to identify what each question is about, which strengthens understanding. It is harder in the moment, but more effective over time.

## A practical method on KamiQuiz

Here is a simple way to improve on a series, applying these principles:

1. **Start with a game in Free play mode**, without reviewing. It gives you an honest starting point and shows you your gaps.
2. **Read the explanations for your mistakes** in the end-of-game summary, then browse the series' review page, where every question is sorted by theme.
3. **Play again a few days later.** The quiz favors questions you have not seen yet, which keeps you from memorizing the order rather than the content.
4. **Target your weak spots** with the By theme mode, choosing the categories where you make the most mistakes.
5. **Finish with Expert or Ascension mode** once your score is regularly above 80%: the hard questions consolidate what you already know.

Enjoyment matters too. We remember best what interests us, and a quiz on a series you love is one of the few memory exercises you actually want to do again. That is exactly why the method works.
